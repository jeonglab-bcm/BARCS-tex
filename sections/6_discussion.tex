Pooled CRISPR screens are no longer limited to comparing two conditions.
Time courses, sorted fractions, donor cohorts, and factorial treatments are
now common, but the beta-binomial approach that suits guide counts had been
available only for two-group comparisons.  Here we extended that approach to
an arbitrary design matrix and showed that BARCS ranks true signals as well as
the most widely used general count models, recovers validated regulators with
a concentrated call set, and keeps gene-level error rates near nominal when
guides share noise.

Two features of the design account for these results.  The first is
dispersion moderation.  A screen typically measures each guide in only a few
independent libraries, so a guide's own dispersion estimate is noisy and its
test statistic inherits that noise.  Moderating the dispersion toward a
library-wide trend raised the essential-gene precision of BARCS in the Cas13
screens from 0.838 to 0.876, the level of limma--voom and edgeR-QL, which
borrow strength across features in the same way.  The second is the
correlation-aware gene statistic.  Because guides that target one gene share
every library, any noise they share makes them less informative than
independent replicates.  BARCS estimates this correlation from residuals, which
a true gene effect does not inflate, and widens the null distribution of the
gene statistic accordingly.  In simulations with correlated guides, this
correction reduced the gene-level type I error from as high as 0.262 to at
most 0.052.  In the real screens we analyzed, the estimated correlation was
0.026 or less, so the independence assumption held approximately; the correction keeps the error rate nominal when guides do share noise,
without requiring negative controls.

The choice of denominator determines what a coefficient means.  Full-library
totals describe changes in library share, and in screens with widespread
depletion this shifts the coefficients of unchanged guides away from zero.
Control-based totals instead describe abundance relative to a chosen control
class, and in the factorial simulations they improved interaction recovery in
every seed.  Non-targeting and safe-harbor controls differ in whether they
absorb the shared cutting response, so the appropriate class depends on the
effect being estimated.  In either case, totals must be fixed before guides are
filtered; recomputing them afterward changes the model without the analyst
intending it, as the external audit of a published false-discovery result
illustrates.

Several limitations remain.  First, BARCS treats each library as independent.
FACS bins partitioned from one donor and repeated harvests from one culture
violate this assumption, and although donor indicators adjust for mean
differences and negative controls correct the resulting miscalibration of
guide tests, as in the IL2RA screen, they do not model the dependence itself;
Waterbear and repeated-measures models address it directly.  Second, the
dispersion enters the covariance as a plug-in estimate, and the residual-$t$
reference accounts for its uncertainty only approximately, although
moderation reduces that uncertainty considerably.  Third, the Cas13 comparison used
deposited values that had already been normalized and batch-corrected, so it
compares methods on a shared input rather than testing either likelihood on
raw counts; the raw reads are available, and our analysis code includes a
pipeline for counting them.

With these considerations in mind, BARCS provides a single framework for
fitting the designs that modern screens use, from time courses to sorted
fractions and factorial treatments, while keeping the sampling model of the
data and reporting how far its gene-level probabilities can be trusted.  BARCS
is available as an R package, and all benchmark code is available to
reproduce the analyses presented here.
